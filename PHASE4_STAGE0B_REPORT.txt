RESULTS

1. Volume evaluated
   579 products, 230 listings (all from Facebook; 145 resolved to a plausible entry, 41 carry a stored product link), 507 reference photos with the
   original listing title, 548 titles filed under 179 products, 167,331 product-name pairs (every pair compared).
   The copy is 55% video games (318 of 579 products) and 11% phones; the other 14 categories together are 26% (about 150 products). That skew matters
   for everything below.

2. Disagreements between the existing matcher and the proposed resolver
   Product-name pairs: 167,331 compared. The existing matcher calls 75 pairs "the same product". For ALL 75 (100%) the proposed resolver says
   "not the same": 60 "one is narrower", 15 "different". It calls only 1 further pair "same" that the old one rejected (iPhone (boxed) = iPhone) and
   flags 2 as a "possible alias" (PlayStation 5 / Ps5, plus a false one, below). In total 78 disagreements (0.047% of all pairs, but 100% of the
   pairs that matter: the ones the old matcher would merge).
   Listings (title -> entry): 144 resolved by both: 134 agree (93%), 10 differ; 13 resolved only by the proposed picker, 1 only by the old rule.
   The stored link on the listing differs from the old rule in 6 cases and from the proposed one in 6.

3. Which side is right? Manual evaluation (my judgement; not proof). I read all 75 pairs and all 11 differing listing picks.
   The 75 pairs the OLD matcher merges:
     - 47 are different products (63%): almost all a game or accessory merged into its console because the platform name sits inside the title,
       e.g. "Pokémon Y (Nintendo 3DS)" = "Nintendo 3DS" (26 such pairs just for the 3DS), "Super Smash Bros. Ultimate (Nintendo Switch)" = "Nintendo switch",
       "Power cable for Xbox Series S" = "Xbox Series S", "Seagate 1TB Storage Expansion Card for Xbox Series X|S" = "Xbox Series S 1TB",
       "Microsoft Xbox One S Console (...)" = "Xbox Wireless Controller", "Nintendo Switch Dock" = "Nintendo switch"; also New Nintendo 3DS XL = Nintendo
       3DS XL, Nintendo 3DS LL = Nintendo 3DS, Nintendo 3DS = New Nintendo 3DS.
     - 16 are the same product (21%): descriptor differences ("Apple iPhone 11" / "iPhone 11", "Handmade fordite keychain" / "Fordite keychain").
     - 12 are genuinely unclear (16%): the Fortnite bundle, a V2 revision, a Limited Edition, (PRODUCT)RED, PS5 Slim digital-edition bundles.
     So the old matcher's wrong-merge rate on these pairs is 63% (47/75), up to 79% if every unclear pair is counted wrong.
   The proposed resolver on the same 75: it correctly refused all 47 wrong merges, but it also refused all 16 correct ones. It is far too strict to be
   allowed to merge (exact recall 0 of 16-28); its value is as a doubt signal and a candidate ranker.
   Listing picks (11 differing, my judgement): proposed better in 6 (iPhone 15+ -> Plus, iPhone 16e, the 512GB entry, Xbox One X, a game lot no longer
   filed as a game), worse in 1 (see bugs), both wrong in 3 (garbled titles such as "Xbox one Series S"), neutral in 1. Of the 13 listings only the proposed
   picker resolved, about 11 are right (mostly "PlayStation 5" / "Ps5", which the old rule skips because its single-digit "5" is dropped) and 2 wrong.
   Uncertainty: no ground truth exists. These are my readings of names; two reviewers could differ on the 12 unclear pairs and on near-synonyms.

5. Estimated prevalence of mixed Database entries (three imperfect proxies; real comps titles are not stored, so this cannot be measured directly)
   a. From the titles filed under each entry: only 13 of 579 entries (2.2%) have 4+ distinct titles, because most have one photo. 1 was flagged and that
      flag is a false positive (split on "sony" vs "nfs"). So this method cannot estimate prevalence on this copy.
   b. Generic entries that have a more specific sibling in the Database: 83 of 579 (14%) at risk (43 video-game, 25 phone). For 14 pairs, titles filed
      under the generic entry state the sibling's distinguishing words; by my reading about 8 are real misfilings (a Meta Quest 3S under Meta Quest 3,

      colorless/capacity-less entries, a PS5 disc drive under PS5), the rest ambiguous or benign. That is a confirmed floor of roughly 1% of entries, with 14%
      "at risk". Value divergence: of 168 generic/specific pairs where both have a price, 136 differ by 30% or more and 124 by 50% or more; many of those are
      game-vs-console pairs that were never the same product, so it is a measure of exposure, not of mispricing.
   c. Entries the old matcher cannot tell apart from another (lookup collisions): 77 of 579 (13%). Worst: Nintendo 3DS (17 twins), New Nintendo 3DS (17),

   My estimate: at least 1-2% of entries are already mixed or misfiled, plausibly several percent, and up to about 14% carry the structural risk.

9. IMPORTANT FINDING ABOUT STAGE 1 (my earlier work), found only because of this shadow run
   The slice-1 "unconfirmed model number" rule would fire on 9 of the 145 listings that match an entry (6.2%), and only 1 of the 9 is justified:
     justified: "iPhone 16e" vs entry "iPhone 16".
     not justified (8): seller inventory codes on Xbox Series S listings (O1766, T3586, JTM7, Z7UX, D6306), the ordinal "13th" (Friday the 13th), the game
     title "2k27" in an NBA 2K27 bundle, and "ps5" against an entry written "PlayStation 5".
   Effect today: such a listing is downgraded from a possible deal to "Worth a look" and, when it would have been pushed, a WatchCount lookup is attempted
   for a nonsense name ("Xbox Series S O1766"). That is roughly 8 wasted lookups per 145 matching listings against a limit of about 20 a day, and a real
   risk of missing real Series S bargains. It is not a safety risk (it errs toward caution and keeps the listing visible), but it is a precision defect.
   I did NOT change it (you limited this stage to validation). Recommended fix, small and contained (Stage 1.1): require a code to be corroborated
   before it counts (seen in the titles of 2+ unrelated listings or comps, or present in a sibling entry's name), treat ordinals and known abbreviations
   as equal ("13th" is not a code; "ps5" = "playstation 5" once an alias exists), and skip codes that appear only once in the whole data set.

STAGE 3a: IS IT SAFE TO BEGIN?
------------------------------
  The schema work itself (additive nullable columns, an aliases table, a background backfill that writes only the new columns) is safe in principle: it
  changes no existing row, and the shadow run showed the data is small enough (579 products) that backfill is cheap. But the results change what Stage 3a
  should be, so my answer is: YES, conditionally, and not exactly as originally planned.
  Conditions:
   1. Do Stage 1.1 first (it is small): the doubt rule is currently noisy in live use.
   2. The proposed resolver must NOT be used to merge or to pick an entry. On real data it is a good doubt signal (refused all 47 wrong merges) but it
      rejects every correct merge, and its alias inference is unreliable. Stage 3a should store identity attributes and candidate relations only,
      and keep merging/aliasing manual or confirmed (corroborated by multiple listings, or approved by you) with undo.
   3. Add a structural rule the data demands: an entry that is a game, accessory or part that merely NAMES its platform ("X (Nintendo 3DS)",
      "Power cable for X") is not the platform. This is the biggest source of wrong merges (26 of 47 are 3DS games alone) and the design must express it
      generically (a name with extra stated content is narrower, never equal), not with a games rule.
   4. Test the migration on a copy of your Database first (I have one now), compare before/after hashes of every pre-existing table, and keep the new
      behavior behind flags, off by default.
   5. Build the per-listing index before any live use (3.5 ms per listing unindexed is too slow to call per candidate).
  Not recommended yet: any automatic splitting or re-valuation of existing entries (Stage 6); it needs the review list first.
